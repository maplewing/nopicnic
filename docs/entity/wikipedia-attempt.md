# Attempting the Wikipedia article

A step-by-step for creating **A Hundred Monkeys** on English Wikipedia with the best
realistic chance of surviving. Written August 2026, after the three sources below
turned up — none of which was ever argued at either deletion discussion.

Read `wikidata-items.md` for the entity work that stands regardless of whether this
is ever attempted, and the assistant memory note `ahm-wikipedia-notability` for the
deletion history.

---

## First: should you do this at all

Honest expected value, stated up front so the rest of this document isn't read as
encouragement.

**In favour.** Three substantive independent features about the studio now exist and
are documented. That is a genuine WP:NCORP case, and it is the first time one has
existed. None of the three was seen by either AfD, so this is not a re-run of an
argument already lost.

**Against.** The article has been deleted twice. That history means any recreation
gets more scrutiny than a first attempt would. All three sources are design or
regional business press rather than national general-interest, and a determined
editor can argue — not unreasonably — that they lean heavily on interviews. Reviews
take two to three months. And even a clean acceptance can be followed by a third AfD.

**Realistic odds:** somewhere around even, done carefully. Not better. If that isn't
worth two or three months of intermittent attention, the honest answer is don't — the
Wikidata items and the entity layer already do most of the work the article was
wanted for, and they can't be deleted by a stranger.

**What failure costs.** A decline costs nothing but time. Being seen to push an
undisclosed COI article after two deletions costs more: it invites a block, and it
can get the title create-protected, which forecloses a better-sourced attempt later.
Every instruction below about disclosure exists to avoid that outcome, not to satisfy
etiquette.

---

## The two things that decide it

Everything else is detail.

1. **Sources.** Not how well it is written. Not how true it is. Whether two or three
   sources exist that are independent, reliable, and *about the company*.
2. **Conduct.** Disclose completely, go through review, never touch the article
   directly once it exists. Editors forgive a weak draft. They do not forgive a
   principal quietly writing their own company's article after two deletions.

---

## Step 1 — Declare, before touching anything

`AbstractArtifact` already exists and is disclosed on Wikidata. Wikimedia accounts
are unified, so the same login works on English Wikipedia — **but the Wikidata
disclosure does not carry over.** It needs its own.

**Declare as paid, not merely as conflicted.** You draw a salary from the company
whose article you would be writing, and you'd be writing it in that capacity. The
Wikimedia Terms of Use treat that as a paid contribution, and WP:PAID explicitly
covers employees writing about their employer. Under-declaring is what gets people
blocked; over-declaring costs nothing at all.

On `User:AbstractArtifact` on **en.wikipedia.org**, save:

```
{{paid|employer=A Hundred Monkeys|client=A Hundred Monkeys|article=A Hundred Monkeys}}

I am Eli Altman, Managing Director of A Hundred Monkeys. I have a conflict of
interest on that topic and on Eli Altman, No Picnic Press, and Danny Altman. I use
Articles for Creation for new pages and talk-page edit requests for changes to
existing ones, and I do not edit those articles directly.
```

Do this **first**, and keep it there permanently. A disclosure added after someone
asks reads as a confession.

## Step 2 — Build the source table before writing a word

This is the step people skip, and skipping it is why drafts fail. Wikipedia's
WP:THREE essay makes the point: name your three best sources and let a reviewer judge
them. If the three cannot carry the article, no amount of prose will.

| Source | Author | Date | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| *STEP inside design*, "The One Thing a Corporate Name Shouldn't Be Is…" Vol. 18 No. 4, pp. 74–77 | Dianna Edwards | Jul/Aug 2002 | Four-page feature about the studio |
| *NorthBay biz*, "Monkey Business" | Bill Meagher | Feb 2010 | Reported feature; the writer visited |
| *Communication Arts*, "A Rose Is a Rose Is a Rose" | Sara Breselor | 4 Feb 2015 | Reported studio visit |

Each is independent of the company, published by an outlet with editorial oversight,
and *about A Hundred Monkeys* rather than about naming-in-general. That last property
is the one WP:ORGIND turns on and the one every source at the 2017 AfD lacked.

**A fourth source, useful but easy to misuse.** "Monkey see, monkey sue?", Dinesh
Ramde, *Associated Press*, 26 September 2009 — the trademark suit against 100
Monkeys Inc. of Wauwatosa, Wisconsin. It is independent in a way none of the 2017
sources were: nobody was interviewed, AP reported a court filing.

Two things about it, and the first is a trap worth naming:

- **It ran in around 200 papers, and that makes it one source, not two hundred.**
  WP:NCORP is explicit that the same article republished is a single source. Writing
  "covered in 200 newspapers" in an AfC note invites a correction, and a reviewer who
  has caught you inflating once will read the three features more sceptically. The
  cost of that is much higher than anything the claim would have bought.
- **It is thin and it is one event.** A wire brief about a filing is not in-depth
  coverage of an organisation, and notability does not follow from a single
  newsworthy incident.

Use it in the body, as the citation for the trademark dispute — a real piece of
company history no other source provides — and list it fourth in the source table,
plainly separated from the three features.

**Supporting only, do not lead with it:** the New York Times Magazine piece
("HOW TO; Invent a Brand Name", Josh Rottenberg, 8 April 2001) is a how-to article
that uses the studio as its worked example. It was rejected at the 2017 AfD and that
rejection was roughly correct. Cite it for specific facts; never present it as
evidence of notability.

**Access notes.** STEP inside design ceased publication and the only copy to hand is
a reprint that was on the company's own old website, preserved at the Internet
Archive. That is fine for verification — WP:SOURCEACCESS is explicit that sources
need not be available online or free — but **cite the magazine, not the reprint**, and
do not present a self-hosted copy as an independent host. If you want it airtight,
find a library holding via WorldCat and cite that. Same for NorthBay biz, where the
original URL is dead and the Archive has eleven snapshots.

### One conflict to write around

The sources disagree about when the company began, and a drafter working from memory
will walk into it. AP says the name had been used "since 1994". The 2010 NorthBay
piece says Danny started A Hundred Monkeys in 1992. Our own account — the practice
from 1990 trading as Whatchamacallit, incorporated as A Hundred Monkeys in 1995 — is
correct and is what the entity layer publishes, but **no cited source states it**.

Wikipedia articles assert what their sources support, not what the subject knows. So
do not write "founded in 1990" and cite one of these; either attribute the claim to
the source making it, or write around the date. Getting challenged on the first
sentence is a bad way to start a review.

## Step 3 — Write from the sources, not from what you know

Open the three sources. Write only what they say. If a sentence cannot be traced to
one of them, it does not go in.

This feels backwards and is the single most important discipline in the exercise. The
failure mode is writing the company's story from memory and then hunting for
citations to bolt on, which produces an article whose shape is promotional even when
every individual fact is true.

**Suggested structure**, and short is genuinely better — four hundred well-cited words
survives where twelve hundred padded ones invites a challenge:

- **Lead** — two or three sentences. What it is, where, when founded, what it is
  known for.
- **History** — founding, the 1995 incorporation, the moves, succession to Eli.
- **Approach** — the anti-"pseudoscience" position; all three sources cover it, which
  is exactly the kind of corroboration reviewers like to see.
- **References.**

**Tone.** No "leading", "renowned", "acclaimed", "innovative", "premier". Write the
way an indifferent stranger would. A reviewer scanning for promotional language will
find one adjective and read the whole draft differently afterwards.

## Step 4 — What to leave out

The temptations, in descending order of how much damage they do:

- **The client list from the company website.** Coca-Cola, Nike, Lego, Samsung, Miro,
  The Slanted Door Group — sourced only to your own site, this is both unsourced and
  promotional, and it is the fastest way to make a draft look like marketing. Only
  name clients an independent source names: *STEP* credits Apple Computer, ABC-TV,
  Timberland, Ben & Jerry's and Goodby Silverstein + Partners; the NYT piece documents
  naming Bill Nguyen's company **Seven**. Those are citable. The rest are not.
- **Anything about the books.** *Don't Call It That* and *Run Studio Run* are
  self-published and belong to Eli, not the company. They add nothing here and invite
  a promotional reading.
- **The Igor connection.** WP:NOTINHERITED exists for exactly this, notability does
  not travel through association, and the real story is two people being laid off.
- **Awards, speaking credits, follower counts, "trusted by" claims.**
- **Any use of ahundredmonkeys.com beyond bare uncontroversial facts.** WP:ABOUTSELF
  permits a little; it should be a footnote, not a foundation.

## Step 5 — Draft, and disclose on the draft

Create at **`Draft:A Hundred Monkeys`** — the Draft namespace, never mainspace. Then
on **`Draft talk:A Hundred Monkeys`**, before submitting:

```
{{Connected contributor (paid)|User1=AbstractArtifact|U1-employer=A Hundred Monkeys|U1-client=A Hundred Monkeys|U1-otherlinks=Disclosed at [[User:AbstractArtifact]]}}
```

Then add a short, plain note for the reviewer. This is worth real care, because it is
the thing that separates "third attempt at a deleted page" from "someone who did the
work":

> This topic was deleted at AfD in 2007 and 2017. Both discussions turned on
> WP:ORGIND — the sources presented were articles about naming that quoted the
> company's principals, rather than coverage of the company itself. This draft is
> built on three sources that were not raised in either discussion and are not of
> that kind: a four-page feature in *STEP inside design* (2002), a reported feature
> in *NorthBay biz* (2010), and a reported studio visit published by *Communication
> Arts* (2015). I have a conflict of interest and have disclosed it; I am submitting
> through AfC rather than creating the article directly.

Naming the prior deletions yourself matters. A reviewer will find them in about
fifteen seconds, and finding them unmentioned changes how they read everything else.

It also addresses the **WP:G4 risk** — speedy deletion of a recreation of deleted
content. G4 applies to pages substantially identical to the deleted version. A draft
built from three sources that version never cited is not that, and saying so plainly
removes the ambiguity. AfC acceptance by an experienced reviewer largely inoculates
against G4 afterwards.

## Step 6 — Submit and wait

Add `{{subst:submit}}` at the top of the draft, or use the "Submit for review" button.

Then wait. There are roughly 3,200 pending submissions and typical waits run **two to
three months**. This is normal and not a signal about your draft. Do not resubmit to
get attention, and do not ask reviewers to hurry.

## Step 7 — If it's declined

Expect this; most drafts are declined at least once, and a decline is cheap.

- **Declined** means "not yet" — read the reason, fix precisely that, resubmit once.
- **Rejected** means "not ever". If you get this on notability grounds, stop.

**Stop after two declines on notability.** At that point the three sources have been
weighed by two independent reviewers and found wanting, and further resubmission
stops being persistence and becomes the behaviour that gets accounts blocked and
titles create-protected. Deletion Review is not the answer either; it exists to
review process errors, not to re-argue sourcing.

Stopping is not defeat. It means the sources aren't there yet, which is a fact about
the world and not about the draft. A fourth source of the same kind would reopen it.

## Step 8 — If it's accepted

Two rules, permanently:

1. **Never edit the article directly again.** Use `{{edit COI}}` on its talk page to
   propose changes and let an uninvolved editor make them. This is slower and it is
   the price of the article existing.
2. **Expect a third AfD.** Given the history, someone may nominate it. The defence is
   the source table — the same three, presented plainly — plus the fact that an
   uninvolved AfC reviewer accepted it. Do not argue at length, do not respond to
   every comment, and do not canvass.

---

## What this is not

This will not make the article permanent, and no approach can. Wikipedia articles
about small firms sit at the mercy of whoever wanders past. What this approach does
is remove every avoidable reason to delete it, so that the only remaining question is
the one you cannot control: whether three good sources are enough for the editors who
show up.

Which is why the Wikidata items and the entity layer were done first. Those are
working now, they cannot be deleted by a stranger, and they carry most of the value
the article was wanted for.
